Day 3

Sutton Grammar 11+ Daily Practice

Maths & English — about 40–45 minutes total

Maths ~20 min English ~20–25 min Try every question on your own first!
🧮

Maths

~20 minutes
Part 1 — Ratio Warm-up

Answer these quickly — mentally or with brief working:

  1. A bag contains 4 red counters and 6 blue counters. Write the ratio of red to blue counters in its simplest form.
    Answer: 2:3
    4:6 — divide both numbers by their highest common factor, 2, to get 2:3.
  2. Share £40 between two people in the ratio 3:5. How much does each person get?
    Answer: £15 and £25
    Total parts: 3 + 5 = 8. One part = 40 ÷ 8 = £5. So 3 parts = £15 and 5 parts = £25.
  3. Simplify the ratio 12:18.
    Answer: 2:3
    The highest common factor of 12 and 18 is 6. 12 ÷ 6 = 2, 18 ÷ 6 = 3.
  4. A recipe uses flour and sugar in the ratio 5:2. If 200 g of sugar is used, how much flour is needed?
    Answer: 500 g
    2 parts = 200 g, so 1 part = 100 g. Flour is 5 parts: 5 × 100 = 500 g.
  5. The ratio of boys to girls in a class is 4:5. There are 27 children in total. How many girls are there?
    Answer: 15 girls
    Total parts: 4 + 5 = 9. One part = 27 ÷ 9 = 3. Girls = 5 parts = 5 × 3 = 15.
Part 2 — Percentages Word Problems

Show clear working for each question:

  1. Find 35% of 160.
    Answer: 56
    10% of 160 = 16, so 30% = 48. 5% = 8. 30% + 5% = 48 + 8 = 56.
  2. A jacket costs £60. In a sale it is reduced by 20%. What is the sale price?
    Answer: £48
    10% of £60 = £6, so 20% = £12. £60 − £12 = £48.
  3. Tom's height increased from 140 cm to 154 cm over a year. What was the percentage increase?
    Answer: 10%
    Increase = 154 − 140 = 14 cm. As a percentage of the original: 14 ÷ 140 = 0.10 = 10%.
  4. A shop increases the price of a £25 toy by 8%. What is the new price?
    Answer: £27
    1% of £25 = £0.25, so 8% = 8 × 0.25 = £2. New price = £25 + £2 = £27.
  5. A £40 pair of trainers is reduced by 15% in a sale. A loyalty card then gives a further £2 off. What is the final price?
    Answer: £32
    10% of £40 = £4, 5% = £2, so 15% = £6 off. £40 − £6 = £34. Then £34 − £2 = £32.

English

~20–25 minutes
Part 1 — Vocabulary
  1. Choose the correct prefix to complete the word so that it means “not possible”:
    ___possible — A) un-    B) im-    C) dis-
    Answer: B) im-
    Before words starting with “p”, the prefix meaning “not” usually becomes “im-”, as in impossible or impatient.
  2. Add the correct suffix to change the verb “act” into a noun meaning “a person who performs”:
    act___ — A) -tion    B) -or    C) -ful
    Answer: B) -or (actor)
    The suffix “-or” turns a verb into a noun that names a person who does that action, e.g. act → actor.
  3. The prefix “auto-” means “self”. Knowing this, what does the word “autograph” mean?
    A) a self-portrait    B) a person's own signature, written by themselves    C) a self-driving car
    Answer: B
    “Auto-” means self, and the root “-graph” means to write. So “autograph” literally means “self-written” — a person's own signature.
  4. Choose the word that best completes the sentence:
    The little boy was ___ when his balloon slipped from his fingers and floated away.
    A) devastated    B) annoyed    C) puzzled
    Answer: A) devastated
    For a young child losing a balloon, “devastated” captures the strong, dramatic sadness far better than the milder words “annoyed” or “puzzled”.
  5. Choose a synonym for enormous:
    A) tiny    B) gigantic    C) narrow
    Answer: B) gigantic
    A synonym has a similar meaning. Gigantic ≈ enormous, both meaning very large.
Part 2 — Poetry Comprehension

Read this poem carefully, then answer the questions:

October Gold

The maple leaves turn gold and red,
And drift like whispers overhead.
The squirrels dart from branch to branch,
Their cheeks packed full with one last chance.

A misty morning wraps the lane,
The air smells sweet with autumn rain.
The apples fall with gentle thuds,
While pumpkins hide among the buds.

The days grow short, the evenings chill,
Yet colour blazes on the hill.
I pull my scarf up to my chin,
And breathe the frosty air within.
  1. Which season is being described in the poem? Give two pieces of evidence from the text.
    Answer: Autumn
    Evidence includes the maple leaves turning “gold and red”, apples falling, pumpkins, and the days growing “short” and evenings turning “chill”.
  2. Find and copy a simile from the first verse.
    Answer: “drift like whispers overhead”
    This is a simile because it uses “like” to compare the falling leaves to whispers.
  3. What rhyming pattern does this poem follow? Give one example rhyming pair.
    Answer: Rhyming couplets (AABB)
    Each pair of lines rhymes, for example “red / overhead” or “branch / chance”.
  4. Why do you think the poet chose the word “whispers” to describe the falling leaves, instead of simply saying they “fall quietly”?
    Example answer: “Whispers” makes the leaves seem gentle and almost alive, as if they are secretly talking, which creates a softer and more magical image than just saying they fall quietly.
  5. Which line or image in the poem do you like best, and why? Answer in one or two sentences.
    Example answer: I like “colour blazes on the hill” because the word “blazes” makes the autumn leaves sound as bright and exciting as fire, even though the poem also talks about cold, chilly evenings.
Part 3 — Writing Task
Prompt: You have just stepped off a train in a town you have never visited before. Describe what you see, hear and feel as you look around for the first time.

Write 8–10 sentences. Try to use:
  • Full stops and commas correctly
  • At least three descriptive adjectives or a simile
  • Varied sentence openings (not every sentence starting with “I”)
Example answer:
The station was smaller than I had imagined, with hanging baskets of flowers swinging gently by the entrance. Steam curled from a nearby food stall, carrying the rich smell of roasted chestnuts across the platform. Unfamiliar voices chattered around me, and somewhere a bell rang twice. Cautiously, I stepped onto the cobbled street, my suitcase rattling behind me like a nervous puppy. Tall, narrow houses leaned over the road, their windows glowing amber in the fading light. “Welcome to Ashbury,” read a faded sign swinging above a bakery door. My heart thudded with a mixture of excitement and nerves. Slowly, I began to walk, wondering what adventures this strange new town might hold.

Check your own writing for: capital letters and full stops, commas in the right places, at least three vivid adjectives or a simile, and sentences that don't all start the same way.
