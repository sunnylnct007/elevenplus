Text A — Information article

Lighthouses: Lonely Lamps of the Coast

an information article
For thousands of years, sailors have feared the dark. Long before maps or motors, a ship creeping home at night could be dashed to pieces on rocks it could not see. To guide these vessels safely, people built lighthouses — tall towers crowned with a burning light.   The very first great lighthouse rose in Egypt, over two thousand years ago. The Pharos of Alexandria stood taller than a modern tower block, and its fire could be seen for miles out to sea. Centuries later, a clever Frenchman named Augustin Fresnel invented a special lens that gathered scattered light into one dazzling beam. His invention, first used in France, was soon copied around the world. Engineers in Scotland built towers that could survive the fiercest storms, and America dotted its long coastline with hundreds of them.   But how does a lighthouse actually work? The idea is surprisingly simple: 1. A powerful lamp burns brightly at the very top of the tower. 2. Around the lamp sits a Fresnel lens, made of rings of polished glass. 3. The lens bends the scattered rays into a single, concentrated beam. 4. A motor slowly turns the lens, so the beam sweeps around the horizon. 5. Each lighthouse flashes in its own pattern, so sailors can tell one from another.   In the past, a keeper lived beside the light and never let it fail. Through every long vigil he trimmed the smoking wick, polished the salty glass, wound the heavy clockwork, recorded the weather in his log, and kept watch until dawn. It was lonely work, but lives depended on it. Sailors called the lighthouse a faithful, flashing, far-off friend.   Today almost every lighthouse is automatic, and the keepers have gone. Yet on a treacherous coast, when a gale howls and the waves rise, that steady sweeping light still whispers the same old promise: don't come closer — you're safe if you stay away.
Text B — A narrative

The Longstone Light

an extract from a story, set on the wild English coast in 1838
The storm woke me before dawn. Rain rattled the little window of the lighthouse, and the wind screamed around the rock like a living thing. I pressed my face to the cold glass and peered into the grey murk. There, upon the jagged reef, lay the broken shape of a wrecked steamer, and clinging to it were people — small, dark figures waving for their lives.   My heart was in my mouth. I ran to wake my father. “We must go to them,” I cried. He shook his head. “The sea is too wild, Grace. No lifeboat could reach them in this.” But I could not leave those poor souls to drown. Without a moment's hesitation, I pulled on my heavy cloak, seized an oar, and helped my father drag our little coble down to the churning water.   What followed I shall never forget. We battled against the tide, the waves towering above our tiny boat, the freezing spray blinding us at every stroke. Twice I thought we would be swept under. Yet somehow we reached the reef, and one by one we hauled the shivering survivors aboard.   By the time we struggled back to the lighthouse, my arms were numb and my hands were raw. But nine people who would have perished were alive and warm before our fire. I was only a keeper's daughter, yet that morning I learned that courage is not the absence of fear — it is doing what is right in spite of it.

1.What type of text is Text A? Find FIVE pieces of evidence to support your answer.[3]

ⓘ Model answer: Text A is an information / explanatory article (non-fiction). Evidence: (1) it gives factual history — “The very first great lighthouse rose in Egypt, over two thousand years ago”; (2) it uses the present tense to state facts — “A powerful lamp burns brightly at the very top”; (3) it names real people and inventions — “Augustin Fresnel invented a special lens”; (4) it asks and then answers a question to explain a process — “But how does a lighthouse actually work?” followed by numbered steps 1–5; (5) it has no story or invented characters — it addresses the reader directly to inform. (A clear title and factual, organised paragraphs also show it is non-fiction.)
2.What do these words mean as they are used in Text A: ‘vigil’, ‘treacherous’ and ‘automatic’?[3]

ⓘ Model answer: vigil — a long period of staying awake and keeping watch, especially at night (“Through every long vigil he trimmed the smoking wick”). treacherous — dangerous and full of hidden risks (“on a treacherous coast…the waves rise”). automatic — working by itself, without a person to operate it (“almost every lighthouse is automatic, and the keepers have gone”).
3.Which FOUR countries are referred to in Text A?[2]

ⓘ Model answer: Egypt (“the very first great lighthouse rose in Egypt”), France (Fresnel's lens “first used in France”), Scotland (“Engineers in Scotland built towers”) and America (“America dotted its long coastline”).
4.In Text A the words ‘do not’ have become ‘don't’. What grammar rule has been applied, and what is the missing letter replaced by?[1]

ⓘ Model answer: A contraction has been used. Two words (“do not”) are shortened into one, and the missing letter (the “o” of “not”) is replaced by an apostrophe — an apostrophe of omission.

ⓘ Model answer: “you're” (“you're safe if you stay away”). Here “you are” is contracted to “you're”, with the apostrophe replacing the omitted letter “a” of “are”.

Evidence: (1) it is told in the first person and past tense — “The storm woke me before dawn”; (2) it has named characters — the narrator “Grace” and her father; (3) it includes direct speech / dialogue — “We must go to them,” I cried; (4) it tells events in order as a plot — the storm, spotting the wreck, and the rescue; (5) it uses descriptive and figurative language — “the wind screamed around the rock like a living thing”. ⓘ Model answer: A coble is a small rowing boat (a little open fishing boat). The text calls it “our little coble” and “our tiny boat”, which Grace and her father rowed out to the wreck.

She herself says “courage is not the absence of fear — it is doing what is right in spite of it”, showing she acted bravely despite being afraid.
