Text A — Information article

The Sweet Science of Honey

an information article
Honey is one of nature's oldest treats, and bees have been making it for millions of years. Long before people learned to keep tidy wooden hives, wild bees stored their golden harvest in hollow trees and rocky crevices, and our ancestors risked painful stings to steal a share of it.   A honeybee colony is a busy city of thousands. Each hive contains one queen, a few hundred male drones, and many thousands of female workers. It is the tireless workers who make the honey, and they scarcely seem to rest from dawn until dusk.   Making honey begins out in the fields. Busy bees buzz between the blossoms, using a long tongue called a proboscis to sip a sugary liquid called nectar from deep inside each flower. A single worker may visit a thousand flowers in one trip, yet all her effort produces only a tiny drop.   Back at the hive the real magic happens: 1. The forager passes the nectar to a house bee, mouth to mouth, again and again. 2. Special chemicals inside the bees' bodies slowly turn the watery nectar into thick syrup. 3. The bees fan the syrup with their beating wings to drive off the extra water. 4. When it is ready, they seal each little cell with a lid of wax to keep the honey fresh.   Why do bees gather so much? They don't make honey for us at all. Honey is their winter food store, and a strong colony can collect far more than it needs. A careful beekeeper takes only the surplus, leaving plenty behind for the bees themselves.   Honey almost never spoils. Sealed jars of it, thousands of years old, have been found in ancient Egyptian tombs, still sweet enough to eat. So the next time you're enjoying a spoonful, remember the countless miles those workers flew to make it.
Text B — A diary

The Diary of an Evacuee, 1940

written by a boy sent away from London during the war
Tuesday, 3rd September 1940   Today I left London on a crowded, clattering train, with a brown label tied to my coat and a gas mask in a cardboard box hung around my neck. Mother did not come with me. She stood on the platform, waving, until the smoke swallowed her up, and I promised myself I would not cry.   We rattled north for hours. When at last we arrived, the village folk lined up in the hall to choose us, as though we were puppies at a market. A tall farmer's wife named Mrs Pettigrew picked me last of all, yet her kitchen smells of fresh bread and her little cottage is warmer than home.   Everything here is strange. There are no motor cars roaring down the lane, no sirens wailing in the dark, and no searchlights sweeping the sky. Instead I hear owls at night and cockerels at dawn. I have seen a real cow being milked, gathered warm eggs from under a hen, and drunk milk that did not come from a tin.   Wednesday, 4th September   I miss Mother terribly. At supper I could not finish my plate, and Mrs Pettigrew kindly pretended not to notice. Later she gave me a candle for my room and said, "Homesickness passes, lad." I do hope she is right. I shall keep this diary until the war is over, and one day I will read it to my own children and tell them how brave we tried to be.

1.What type of text is Text A? Find FIVE pieces of evidence to support your answer.[3]

ⓘ Model answer: Text A is an information / explanation (non-fiction) article. Evidence: (1) it gives factual information — “bees have been making it for millions of years”; (2) it is written in the present tense to state facts — “Honey is one of nature's oldest treats”; (3) it uses technical, subject-specific vocabulary — “proboscis”, “nectar”, “drones”; (4) it gives numbers and quantities — “one queen, a few hundred male drones, and many thousands of female workers”; (5) it explains a process in numbered steps (steps 1–4). There is no invented story or characters, which shows it is non-fiction.
2.What do these words mean as they are used in Text A: ‘proboscis’, ‘surplus’ and ‘crevices’?[3]

ⓘ Model answer: proboscis — the bee's long tongue, used to sip nectar from inside a flower (“a long tongue called a proboscis”). surplus — the extra amount that is left over above what is needed (“takes only the surplus”). crevices — narrow cracks, gaps or splits, here in the rocks (“hollow trees and rocky crevices”).
3.According to Text A, list the FOUR steps the bees take to turn nectar into honey once it reaches the hive.[2]

ⓘ Model answer: (1) The forager passes the nectar to a house bee, mouth to mouth, again and again; (2) special chemicals inside the bees' bodies slowly turn the watery nectar into thick syrup; (3) the bees fan the syrup with their wings to drive off the extra water; (4) they seal each cell with a lid of wax to keep the honey fresh.
4.In Text A the words ‘do not’ have become ‘don't’. What grammar rule has been applied, and what are the missing letters replaced by?[1]

ⓘ Model answer: A contraction has been used. Two words (“do not”) are shortened into one, and the missing letter (the “o” of “not”) is replaced by an apostrophe — an apostrophe of omission.

ⓘ Model answer: “you're” (“the next time you're enjoying a spoonful”). Here “you are” is contracted to “you're”, with the apostrophe replacing the omitted letter “a” of “are”.

8.What do these phrases mean as used in Text B: ‘the smoke swallowed her up’, ‘as though we were puppies at a market’ and ‘Homesickness passes’?[3]

ⓘ Model answer: the smoke swallowed her up — the train's smoke hid his mother from view completely, so he could no longer see her. as though we were puppies at a market — the children were looked over and picked out by strangers, just like animals for sale, with no say in the matter. 9.Find THREE pieces of evidence from Text B that show the boy is homesick or missing home.[3]

ⓘ Model answer: (1) “I miss Mother terribly” — he says plainly that he is missing his mother; (2) “At supper I could not finish my plate” — he is too upset to eat; (3) he “promised myself I would not cry” when the train left, showing he was close to tears (and Mrs Pettigrew has to reassure him that “Homesickness passes”).
10.What is a ‘searchlight’, and why would there be none in the countryside?[1]

ⓘ Model answer: A searchlight is a powerful beam of light that was swept across the night sky to spot enemy aircraft during the war (“no searchlights sweeping the sky”). There are none in the quiet countryside because it is far from the city that was being bombed, so it is safer and there are no air raids to defend against.
11.Explain why the writer feels the countryside is ‘strange’ compared with London.[1]

ⓘ Model answer: It feels strange because it is so different from the noisy, dangerous city he knows: there are “no motor cars roaring down the lane, no sirens wailing in the dark, and no searchlights sweeping the sky”. Instead there are country sights and sounds he has never met before — owls, cockerels, a cow being milked and fresh milk that does not come from a tin.

ⓘ Model answer: (1) heard owls at night; (2) heard cockerels at dawn; (3) seen a real cow being milked; (4) gathered warm eggs from under a hen; and (5) drunk milk that did not come from a tin.
