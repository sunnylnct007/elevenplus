Text A — Information article

Making Magnificent Mosaics

an information and how-to article
A mosaic is a picture or pattern made from many small pieces of coloured material pressed into place. The art is astonishingly old. The ancient Greeks first laid patterns of dark and light pebbles across the floors of their grand houses in Greece, and the Romans later perfected the craft, covering the walls and floors of villas throughout Italy with tiny cubes of stone and glass called tesserae. Wherever their empire spread, the Romans carried the fashion with them, and mosaics never went out of style.   In Morocco, skilled craftsmen still chip glazed tiles into dazzling geometric stars that decorate fountains, doorways and shady courtyards. Centuries later, in the Spanish city of Barcelona, the architect Antoni Gaudí covered benches, walls and rooftops with a riot of broken tiles, proving that even smashed rubbish can transform a dull surface into something truly wonderful.   Making a mosaic of your own is easier than you might think, and you will not need to spend a fortune. Broken bits become beautiful in the hands of a patient maker. Begin by collecting broken crockery — chipped plates, cracked cups and old saucers are perfect. You'll also need a pair of tile cutters to snip the pieces to size, a sturdy table to work on, some strong glue, a tub of grout, and an old cloth or buff rag for polishing.   Once your materials are ready, follow these steps: 1. Sketch a simple design onto your base board so that you know where each colour should go. 2. Place the tiles, one by one, onto the glue, leaving a small recess or gap between them. 3. Press each piece down firmly and let the glue dry completely overnight. 4. Apply the grout, spreading it into every gap with your fingers until the cracks are filled. 5. Let the grout dry until it turns pale and firm. 6. Clean off the excess grout with your buff cloth, rubbing gently until every tile gleams.   Do not rush the drying stages — if you can't wait, the grout will smear and your picture will be spoiled. A mosaic made with care can last for thousands of years, as the Roman floors still buried beneath modern Italy prove to this very day. So gather your broken crockery, and begin!
Text B — A letter

A Letter from the Nail Works, 1842

written by a child nail-maker to her brother
Dear Thomas, I take up my pen to tell you how things stand at home, for I know you worry about us all. I am but twelve years old, yet I have worked at the nail block since I was eight. Each morning I rise before dawn and stand at the hearth for fourteen hours, hammering out nails until my arms ache and my eyes sting from the smoke. For all this labour I earn only a few pennies a week — scarcely enough to make ends meet. The foggers are the worst of our troubles. A fogger is a middleman who hands out the iron and buys back our finished nails, yet he pays us in goods from his own shop instead of honest coin, and cheats us on the weight besides. However hard we toil, he grows fat while we grow thin. Lately things have become far worse. Cheap nails made by machine, which the men here call "Belgiums", have flooded the market from across the sea. Because a machine can make in an hour what takes me a whole day, no one will pay a fair price for our hand-made nails. Many of my friends have gone hungry: some could find no work at all, some were paid only in spoiled flour they could not sell, and some grew too weak from want of food to lift the hammer. Last week something just snapped. A crowd of nailers gathered in the square and began causing havoc. They smashed the windows of the fogger's shop, set fire to his warehouse, broke the new machines to pieces, marched through the streets with burning torches, and seized loaves of bread from the baker's cart. I was frightened, but a part of me understood their anger. We cannot go on like this much longer. Pray for us, and write when you are able. Your loving sister, Mary
