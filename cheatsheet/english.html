English

Kent Paper 2 splits English into comprehension, spelling, punctuation, and word choice. Each rewards a slightly different skill — comprehension is about evidence, the rest are about noticing.

Comprehension technique Reading

  1. Read the whole passage once before looking at the questions — don't hunt line-by-line from question 1.
  2. Literal questions ("Where did X happen?") — the answer is stated directly; find and quote it.
  3. Inference questions ("How does the character feel?") — the answer isn't written directly; use clues (word choice, actions) to work it out.
  4. Vocabulary-in-context ("What does 'lulled' mean here?") — re-read the sentence around the word; the surrounding context almost always confirms or rules out each option.
  5. "Find and copy a phrase…" — copy exactly as printed, don't paraphrase.
Point → Evidence → Explain (PEE): make the point, quote the line that proves it, then explain why that line proves it. Even one sentence of "explain" turns a vague answer into a strong one.
Worked example — literal vs inference, side by side
Passage: "Tom's hands trembled as he opened the envelope. He had waited three months for this letter."

Literal question: "How long had Tom waited for the letter?" — the answer is stated word-for-word: three months.
Inference question: "How does Tom feel as he opens the envelope?" — nowhere does it say "nervous", but "hands trembled" is a physical clue that points to nervous / anxious.
Common mistake: answering an inference question by just re-reading the literal facts. If a question asks "how" or "why" someone feels or acts, the examiner wants you to interpret a clue, not quote a fact.

Word classes (parts of speech) Grammar

Knowing what job a word is doing in a sentence underpins almost every SPaG question — spotting the verb, or telling an adjective from an adverb, comes up constantly.

Word classJobExample
Nouna person, place or thingchildren, garden
Verban action or stateran, is, thinks
Adjectivedescribes a nounexcited children
Adverbdescribes a verb (often ends -ly)ran quickly
Pronounstands in for a nounit, she, they
Conjunctionjoins two parts of a sentenceand, but, because
Prepositionshows position/relationshipoutside the house
Worked example
"The excited children quickly ran outside because it had stopped raining."
excited = adjective (describes children) · quickly = adverb (describes ran) · ran = verb · because = conjunction (joins the two ideas).
The same word can be different word classes in different sentences — "light" is a noun in "turn on the light" but an adjective in "a light bag". Always check the job it's doing in that sentence.

Sentence types & clauses Grammar

There are four sentence types by purpose: statement (tells you something, ends in a full stop), question (asks something, ends in ?), command (an instruction, e.g. "Close the door."), exclamation (shows strong feeling, ends in !).

Sentences also come in three structures, built from clauses (a clause is a group of words with its own verb):

StructureMade fromExample
Simpleone main clauseThe dog barked.
Compoundtwo main clauses joined by and/but/orThe dog barked and the cat ran.
Complexa main clause + a subordinate clauseThe dog barked because it saw a squirrel.
A subordinate clause can't stand alone as a full sentence ("because it saw a squirrel" isn't complete on its own) — that's what marks it out from a main clause.

Worked example
"The old floorboards groaned under his feet." Which technique, and what effect? This is personification (floorboards can't really groan) — it makes the house feel almost alive, adding a slightly eerie atmosphere.

Spelling SPaG

  • i before e, except after c (when the sound is "ee"): believe, receive.
  • Doubling the last consonant before -ing/-ed on a short vowel word: hop → hopping, stop → stopped.
  • -able vs -ible: no fixed rule — if the root is a complete word, it's usually -able (comfortable); learn -ible words individually (possible, terrible).
  • Plurals: usually add -s (cat → cats); add -es after s, x, ch, sh (box → boxes, fox → foxes); change a consonant + y to -ies (baby → babies), but a vowel + y just adds -s (toy → toys).

Words that show up again and again in these papers — learn the whole list:

separate · definitely · necessary · professional · persuade · consistent · compulsory · solicitor · hoping / hopping · receive · announced · grateful · achievement

Homophones (sound the same, spelled differently, mean different things) are a favourite trap:

their / there / they're · its / it's · your / you're · to / too / two · where / wear / we're

Every spelling question here follows the same shape: one wrong word hidden among four groups of words, or no mistake at all (marked N). Read the whole sentence once for meaning, then a second time scanning letter-by-letter.

Punctuation SPaG

RuleExample
Apostrophe for possessionthe dog's bowl
Apostrophe for contractionit's raining (it is)
Comma after a fronted clauseAlthough it was late, we stayed.
Comma before a name/asideJuan, the tallest boy, won.
Speech marks around spoken words"This is unfair," she said.
Question mark for a genuine questionWas she sure? (not "she wasn't sure?")

Colon introduces a list or explanationShe packed three things: a hat, a map, a torch.
Brackets/dashes add extra, droppable infoThe trophy (which was solid gold) gleamed.
Ellipsis shows a trailing-off or pauseI wasn't sure what to say…
A very common trap: a statement written with a question mark, or a question written with a full stop. Read the sentence out loud in your head — does it actually ask something? For brackets/dashes: try reading the sentence with that part removed entirely — it should still make complete grammatical sense either way.

Word choice & vocabulary SPaG

Cloze-style questions ask you to pick the word that fits the sentence's exact meaning, not just a similar one.

Worked example
"We __________ the referee to change the decision." The options include implored (begged strongly), implied (suggested), claimed, imposed, concerned. Only "implored" fits a plea to someone else — the answer is implored.
Worked example — a near-synonym trap
"New laws have __________ the sale of the dangerous chemical." Options: abridged (shortened, e.g. a book), abandoned (given up on), idolised (adored), abolished (officially ended), adroit (skilful, and not even a verb here). Only "abolished" means "made illegal/ended by law" — the answer is abolished.
Worked example — tone matters too
"The critic's review was __________ , praising every detail of the performance." Which fits: scathing (harshly critical) or glowing (full of praise)? The sentence itself tells you the tone with "praising every detail" — the answer is glowing.
  1. Read the whole sentence first, ignoring the gap.
  2. Predict what kind of word is missing (a strong verb? a connector? a positive or negative word?).
  3. Eliminate options that are almost-but-not-quite the right meaning — these papers deliberately include near-synonyms as traps.
