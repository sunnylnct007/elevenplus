Coding shapes Codes

Each shape carries two code letters: one for what it is, one for its shading. Work out which letter means what, then build the code for a new shape.

solid square = PT    outline circle = QS
Worked example
Test shape: outline square. P = square, S = outline → PS.
solid hexagon = EQ    outline pentagon = FR
Another
Test shape: solid pentagon. F = pentagon, Q = solid → FQ.
Line the two example shapes up mentally and ask "what's different between them, and what's the same?" — whatever's the same letter across two different shapes is the code for the property they share (usually the shading), and the rest is the shape code.

3D block assembly & counting cubes Spatial

A target shape is built from identical unit cubes. Five options show different groups of smaller blocks — only one set has the right total number of cubes and the right shape when joined together.

  1. Count the total cubes in the target shape first.
  2. Rule out any option set whose block-pieces don't add up to that total.
  3. For what's left, mentally rotate the pieces — a block can be turned any way, it just can't change shape.
Worked example
The target shape is built from 7 unit cubes. Option A offers pieces of 3, 2 and 2 cubes (3+2+2 = 7 ✓). Option B offers pieces of 3, 3 and 2 cubes (3+3+2 = 8 ✗). Option B is eliminated on the count alone, before you even check whether the shapes would actually fit together — the answer must be Option A (assuming its pieces also fit the target's outline).
Don't be fooled by an option with the right number of blocks but the wrong width/height/depth — always sanity-check the overall outline shape too, not just the block count.

Nets — folding flat shapes into 3D Spatial

A net is a 2D shape that folds up into a 3D solid. For a cube net: opposite faces on the finished cube are always two squares apart in the net, never adjacent.

Opposite pairs once folded: 1↔6, 2↔4, 3↔5 — each pair sits two squares apart along the strip.

A "staircase" net — a completely different-looking layout that also folds into a cube. There are 11 different net shapes that all work, not just the cross.

If a question shows painted/patterned faces on a net and asks which two end up opposite (or touching) once folded, physically trace the fold with your finger on the page — imagining it in your head is where mistakes creep in.

Combining shapes at matching edges Spatial

A base shape has two edges marked x and y. A small shape is added on at the matching-letter edge, and another is added or subtracted at the other. Line up the letters, then work out the resulting outline.

Worked example
A base pentagon has edges marked x and y. A small triangle marked x is added to the pentagon at the x edge. A small semicircle marked y is subtracted from the pentagon at the y edge. The correct final shape is a pentagon with a triangular bump on one side and a semicircular notch cut into a different side — both changes must appear together for an option to be correct. An option showing only the added triangle, or only the missing semicircle, is wrong: it's applied just one of the two instructions.
Do this one step at a time — first add/remove at x, redraw the shape in your head, then add/remove at y. Trying to do both at once is where errors happen.

2D plan views of a 3D shape Spatial

You're shown a 3D object and asked to pick its view from directly above, the side, or the front — like a floor plan or an architect's elevation.

A stepped block, viewed from three different sides — each view only shows the outline, not the depth behind it.

The top (plan) view is the one people find hardest — imagine looking straight down and drawing only what you'd see, ignoring how tall each part is. Two objects of very different heights can have an identical plan view.

Pick one distinctive feature of the target (e.g. "the tail points towards the biggest angle") and track only that feature across the five options — much faster than comparing the whole shape at once.

Fold your answer sheet (or your hand) along the mirror line and check the two sides line up — the most common trap is picking an option that's just a copy of the same shape, facing the same direction, rather than a true reflection.

Rotational symmetry Pattern

A shape has rotational symmetry if it looks exactly the same after being turned by less than a full circle. The order of rotational symmetry is how many times it matches itself during one complete turn.

⬟

A regular pentagon has rotational symmetry of order 5 — turn it by 1/5 of a full circle (72°) and it looks identical, and that happens 5 times in one full turn.

Worked example
What order of rotational symmetry does a square have? A square looks the same after a quarter-turn (90°), a half-turn (180°), a three-quarter-turn (270°), and a full turn (360°) — that's order 4.
For any regular polygon, the order of rotational symmetry always equals its number of sides — the same number you'd use for its lines of symmetry (see the Maths cheat sheet). An irregular shape can have rotational symmetry too, or none at all — a scalene triangle (no equal sides) has order 1, which really means "no rotational symmetry" other than the full 360° turn.
